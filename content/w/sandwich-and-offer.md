I arrived back in Đà Nẵng earlier last week. I hadn't been in town for a couple of years, yet it felt so natural to roam the Sơn Trà neighbourhood again. The central coast of Vietnam welcomed me with steady rainfall, and Sunday was the first morning I woke up to bright sunshine.

I hadn't had a proper Bánh mì Chay in a while and found a stall that I had saved last time I was in town. The shop had since moved, from a busy street to a quaint side alley. I quickly sat down and ordered a couple of the vegetarian sandwiches, a nice breakfast I rarely have.

After finishing my meal and going up to pay the vendor, we started discussing both of our journeys. She quickly shared that she was a Buddhist, which is common among vegan food sellers. One thing led to another, and she told me:

> "Tomorrow I close the shop. I will go with my family to buy fish from the market and release them into the river. Do you want to come with us?"

Pleasantly surprised, I immediately said yes. It felt strange, as the idea of buying fishes from aquarium stores and releasing them in the wild had crossed my mind before.

He's an Asian arowana, about 70 cm long, and lives with a couple of shy friends in his tank.

During the year I lived in this neighbourhood, he swam back and forth in the same aquarium. He was never rescued and never moved from that spot. He lived in the shop window and stared at the same busy street.

I couldn't help but imagine his desires for more than an empty cube of water.

Most days I would walk past and stop to say hi for a few seconds. He would stop his routine laps and turn around, come to face me at the window. I couldn't decipher his thoughts, but the more I saw him, the more I wished for him to be free.

I had him in mind when I agreed to join the quest to free others.

## Getting the fish

On the following day, a cloudy Monday morning, I joined my new friend at her shop. She was waiting for me with her mom, and offered me a coffee as soon as I arrived. We chatted for a bit and I learned more about her intentions with this act.

Her parents were fisherfolk and had spent most of their working lives fishing off the coast of central Vietnam, bringing their catch ashore to sell. She expressed how worried she was for their next life. She felt strongly about the suffering they caused and how it harmed their karma. She wanted to act, following her beliefs, and to bring her family along with her.

It was time to go. First stop: the fish market.

![Live eels and fish in bowls at the covered fish market](/photos/danangfish-market.webp)

We scoured the aisles of the bustling covered hangar to finally find a stall that had live fish for sale. They had what I think were eels (that's the only name I could give to a long fish; I need to learn more about fish).

After a small negotiation, we packed ten kilograms of said eels and a few more fish. An uncle helped me strap the big polystyrene box onto my bike, and off we went.

## To the River

We had a half-hour ride: my friend and her mom on one bike, me and the fish on another. It was a cloudy day. I was worried it might rain. The box blocked access to the bike storage, so I couldn't get my raincoat, and we hurried toward the river.

![The strapped polystyrene box mounted on a scooter](/photos/danangfish-box.webp)

After driving through small villages, we finally got to the river. We unpacked our box in front of a bewildered passerby. After taking our shoes off, we walked down the half-submerged pier. In the distance we could see a beautiful temple overlooking the water. We took a moment to gather ourselves, pray, and release the fish.

![My friend facing the river and the temple in the distance](/photos/danangfish-river.webp)

My friend told me that sometimes locals would try to stop her from releasing the fish, so we had to act quickly.

The deed was done, and indeed, as we walked back up the pier, a couple of villagers came up to us. Too late to stop us, they instead came with plastic bags full of fish. They asked us to buy them for release, but we declined at this time.

Seeing the eels swim back into the river was a relief. I couldn't help but imagine how they felt: stuck in a box for who knows how long, then freed again, back to an unknown river, back in the comfort of their home.
